A/B Testing and Causal Inference: did it really work?¶

The question: every product team asks "did the change cause the improvement?" There are two ways to find out. The gold standard is an A/B test: flip a coin to decide who sees the new page, and compare. When you cannot flip coins (people chose a loyalty programme themselves), you need causal inference on observational data, where a naive comparison is usually wrong. How many visitors does an A/B test need? How often does it fool us, and how does "peeking" make that worse? And when people chose their own treatment, how far off is the naive answer, can propensity-score matching rescue it, and what can it not rescue? Because we simulate both data sets, we know the true answer and can mark every method right or wrong.

1. 💡 The simple analogy¶

Two ways to learn whether a new fertiliser works.

  • The A/B test (a coin flip decides): you toss a coin for every plant to decide whether it gets fertiliser. Sunny spots and shady spots end up in both groups by luck, so any difference in height is the fertiliser. The hard part is how many plants you need to notice a small effect, and not peeking at the heights every morning and stopping the day it looks good.
  • The observational study (the gardener chose): the gardener gave the fertiliser to the plants that already looked healthiest, in the sunniest spots. Comparing the two groups says nothing about the fertiliser: the sunny plants would have grown taller anyway. Matching pairs each fertilised plant with an unfertilised twin in a similarly sunny spot and compares those.
  • The catch: matching can only pair plants on things you recorded. If the gardener also used a secret sense for "plants that feel strong" that you never wrote down, matching cannot remove that part.

3. Hypotheses and why we chose each test¶

# Question H₀ (nothing interesting) Hₐ Test
H1 When the two pages are identical (an A/A test), does the test call a difference 5% of the time, as promised? The false positive rate equals 0.05 It differs 5,000 simulated A/A tests; Wilson interval; Kolmogorov-Smirnov test that the p-values are uniform
H2 Does the power formula predict how often a real +1 point lift is found, and what do underpowered experiments report? Simulated power equals the formula It differs 5,000 simulated experiments at each sample size; the average lift among "winners"
H3 Does checking the running result and stopping at the first p < 0.05 keep the 5% error rate? Still 5% It grows with the number of looks Simulated A/A tests with 1 to 50 looks; Bonferroni correction as the fix
H4 When people chose their own treatment, does the naive difference recover the true +5 euros, and do adjusted estimates? Each estimator is unbiased Naive is biased; adjusted ones are not Bootstrap CIs (the whole pipeline repeated); is the truth inside?
H5 If a cause of both joining and spending is not recorded, can matching still recover the truth? Still unbiased Bias grows with the hidden cause 10 simulated data sets at each strength of the hidden cause

Why these tests? A conversion is a yes-or-no, so the two-proportion z-test (identical to a chi-square test) is the classic choice. We do not trust a method because a textbook says so: we simulate thousands of experiments where we know the truth and count how often it is right. For H4 and H5 the known truth turns "does this method work?" into a number: the bias.

4. Part A: the A/B test, and how big it must be¶

What: the current page converts 10% of visitors and the new page really converts 11% (a minimum detectable effect of +1 point). Power analysis asks: how many visitors per arm do we need so that, if the lift is real, the test detects it 80% of the time (power) while calling a difference when there is none only 5% of the time (alpha)? Why: decide the sample size before the experiment; stopping when the result looks good is the most common way to fool yourself.

def required_n(
    p_control: float, p_variant: float, alpha: float = ALPHA, power: float = POWER
) -> int:
    """Visitors needed in each arm to detect a change in a conversion rate.

    Args:
        p_control: Conversion rate of the control.
        p_variant: Conversion rate the variant is hoped to reach.
        alpha: Allowed false positive rate.
        power: Wanted chance of detecting the change if it is real.

    Returns:
        The number of visitors per arm (two-sided test, equal arms).
    """
    effect = proportion_effectsize(p_variant, p_control)
    return int(
        np.ceil(
            NormalIndPower().solve_power(
                effect, alpha=alpha, power=power, ratio=1.0, alternative="two-sided"
            )
        )
    )


def z_test(x_control: np.ndarray, x_variant: np.ndarray, n: int) -> tuple[np.ndarray, np.ndarray]:
    """Two-proportion z-test for many experiments at once.

    Args:
        x_control: Conversions in the control arm of each experiment.
        x_variant: Conversions in the variant arm of each experiment.
        n: Visitors per arm.

    Returns:
        The two-sided p-value and the observed difference of each experiment.
    """
    p_c, p_v = x_control / n, x_variant / n
    pooled = (x_control + x_variant) / (2 * n)
    se = np.sqrt(np.maximum(pooled * (1 - pooled) * 2 / n, 1e-12))
    return 2 * stats.norm.sf(np.abs((p_v - p_c) / se)), p_v - p_c

  • The cost of precision is steep: halving the lift to be detected (0.5 points) needs 57,756 visitors per arm (about four times as many); a lift of 2 points needs 3,835 and one of 3 points 1,769. The sample size grows with the inverse square of the effect.

5. H1: does an A/A test cry wolf 5% of the time?¶

What: an A/A test shows two identical pages to two groups. Any "significant" difference is a false alarm. We simulate 5,000 A/A experiments of 14,745 visitors per arm, count how often p < 0.05, and check that the p-values are spread evenly between 0 and 1 (as they must be when nothing is going on). Why: before trusting a testing tool, check that it keeps its promise.

In [3]:
def rejection_rate(
    n: int, p_control: float, p_variant: float, sims: int = N_SIMULATIONS
) -> tuple[float, float, float]:
    """Share of simulated experiments with p < alpha, with a 95% Wilson interval."""
    p, _ = z_test(rng.binomial(n, p_control, sims), rng.binomial(n, p_variant, sims), n)
    hits = int((p < ALPHA).sum())
    low, high = proportion_confint(hits, sims, method="wilson")
    return hits / sims, low, high

  • The p-values are uniform: the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test finds no departure from an even spread (p = 0.38). We do not reject H₀: with a fixed sample size and one look, the test does what it says.

6. H2: does the power formula work, and what do underpowered tests report?¶

What: now the new page really is 1 point better. We run 5,000 simulated experiments at sample sizes from 10% to 200% of the planned size and count how often the test detects the lift (simulated power), next to the power formula. We also record the lift reported by the experiments that reached significance (the "winners"). Why: a formula is only useful if it matches reality, and a small experiment that happens to win may be telling a flattering lie.

How to read this:

  • The formula matches the simulation: at the planned size the simulated power is 80.1% (95% CI 78.9% to 81.1%) against 80.0% from the formula; at half the size 49.9% against 50.8%; at twice the size 97.5% against 97.7%. We do not reject H₀.
  • Underpowered tests miss real effects: with a quarter of the planned sample the test finds the real lift only 28.5% of the time (and 14.0% with a tenth).
  • And when they "win", they exaggerate: the experiments that reach significance report an average lift of 1.85 points at a quarter of the planned size (the truth is 1.0): 1.8 times too large; at a tenth of the size, 2.79 points. At the full planned size the winners report 1.13 points, close to the truth. This is the "winner's curse": significance at a small sample selects the lucky overestimates.

7. H3: what does peeking do?¶

What: a very common habit: look at the running result every day and stop the first time p < 0.05. We simulate A/A experiments (no true difference) checked 1, 2, 5, 10, 20 or 50 times over the planned sample, and count how often one of the looks says "significant". We also try the simplest fix, the Bonferroni correction: only stop if p < 0.05 divided by the number of looks. Why: each look is another lottery ticket; the error rate you were promised only holds for one look at the end.

def peeking_false_positive_rate(looks: int, sims: int = N_SIMULATIONS) -> tuple[float, float]:
    """False positive rate of an A/A test checked after each of ``looks`` equal batches.

    The experiment stops (and declares a winner) the first time a look gives p < alpha.

    Args:
        looks: Number of times the result is checked, spread evenly over the planned sample.
        sims: Number of simulated experiments.

    Returns:
        The share stopped early with 'significant' at alpha, and at alpha / looks (Bonferroni).
    """
    batch = N_REQUIRED // looks
    x_c = rng.binomial(batch, BASE_RATE, (sims, looks)).cumsum(1)
    x_v = rng.binomial(batch, BASE_RATE, (sims, looks)).cumsum(1)
    n_seen = batch * np.arange(1, looks + 1)
    p_c, p_v = x_c / n_seen, x_v / n_seen
    pooled = (x_c + x_v) / (2 * n_seen)
    z = (p_v - p_c) / np.sqrt(np.maximum(pooled * (1 - pooled) * 2 / n_seen, 1e-12))
    p = 2 * stats.norm.sf(np.abs(z))
    return float((p < ALPHA).any(1).mean()), float((p < ALPHA / looks).any(1).mean())

How to read this:

  • The false positive rate climbs with every look: 5.8% with one look (the promised 5%, within the noise of 5,000 simulations), 8.4% with 2, 14.0% with 5, 18.9% with 10, 24.2% with 20 and 32.3% with 50. Checking daily for a few weeks makes an identical page a "winner" about one time in five or more. We reject H₀.
  • Bonferroni restores the promise, at a price: with the corrected threshold the rate is 4.1%, 3.0%, 2.6%, 1.6%, 1.2% for 2, 5, 10, 20 and 50 looks: safe, but conservative, so it needs more visitors to detect a real lift. Sequential methods designed for peeking (alpha-spending boundaries) are more efficient; the cleanest rule is still to decide the sample size in advance and look once.

What: run one experiment at the planned size, as a real analyst would: conversions in each arm, the difference with a 95% confidence interval (Newcombe's method, which behaves well for proportions), the z-test and the equivalent chi-square test. Why: a p-value alone says "something is there"; the interval says how big.

9. Part B: causal inference when people chose their own treatment¶

What: a store offers a loyalty programme. Nobody was assigned: customers chose to join. We simulate 20,000 customers: older, longer-standing and bigger-spending customers join more often, and they also spend more anyway (that is confounding). The programme truly raises monthly spend by +5 euros for everybody, which we know because we built the data. We then look at what the naive comparison says. Why: this is the everyday situation of analysts who have data but no experiment.

In [9]:
def make_customers(n: int, hidden_strength: float, gen: np.random.Generator) -> pd.DataFrame:
    """Simulates customers, some of whom join a loyalty programme (nobody was assigned).

    Older, longer-standing and bigger-spending customers are more likely to join (confounding),
    and they also spend more anyway. A hidden trait (``affinity``, not recorded in practice)
    pushes both joining and spending when ``hidden_strength`` is above 0. The programme really
    raises monthly spend by TRUE_EFFECT for everybody, so the right answer is known.

    Args:
        n: Number of customers.
        hidden_strength: How strongly the hidden trait raises the chance of joining.
        gen: Random number generator.

    Returns:
        One row per customer with the recorded covariates, ``treated``, ``spend`` and the hidden
        trait ``affinity`` (kept only so we can check our work).
    """
    age = gen.normal(40, 12, n).clip(18, 80)
    tenure = gen.gamma(2.0, 1.5, n)
    prior_spend = gen.lognormal(4.0, 0.8, n)
    affinity = gen.normal(0, 1, n)
    z_age, z_tenure = (age - 40) / 12, (tenure - 3) / 2.1
    z_spend = (np.log(prior_spend) - 4.0) / 0.8
    logit = -0.6 + 0.6 * z_age + 0.7 * z_tenure + 0.9 * z_spend + hidden_strength * affinity
    treated = gen.random(n) < 1 / (1 + np.exp(-logit))
    spend = (
        60
        + 6 * z_age
        + 5 * z_tenure
        + 14 * z_spend
        + 6 * affinity
        + TRUE_EFFECT * treated
        + gen.normal(0, 18, n)
    )
    return pd.DataFrame(
        {
            "age": age,
            "tenure_years": tenure,
            "prior_spend": prior_spend,
            "log_prior_spend": np.log(prior_spend),
            "treated": treated.astype(int),
            "spend": spend,
            "affinity": affinity,
        }
    )

10. Propensity scores, matching and balance¶

What: the propensity score is each customer's chance of joining, estimated from what we recorded (age, tenure, log of past spend; nothing measured after joining) with a logistic regression. Matching pairs each joiner with the non-joiner whose score is closest (within a caliper of 0.2 standard deviations of the logit score), so we compare people who were equally likely to join. We check overlap (are there non-joiners like the joiners?) and balance (after matching, are the groups alike?), using the standardised mean difference (SMD; below 0.1 counts as balanced). Why: matching only works if the groups overlap, and it only helps if balance is actually achieved.

def propensity_scores(frame: pd.DataFrame, columns: list[str]) -> np.ndarray:
    """Each customer's chance of joining, estimated from the recorded covariates only.

    Args:
        frame: Customers.
        columns: The covariates to use (nothing measured after the decision to join).

    Returns:
        The estimated probability of joining for each customer.
    """
    model = make_pipeline(StandardScaler(), LogisticRegression(C=1e6, max_iter=1000))
    return model.fit(frame[columns], frame["treated"]).predict_proba(frame[columns])[:, 1]


def match(
    frame: pd.DataFrame, ps: np.ndarray, caliper_sd: float = CALIPER_SD
) -> tuple[np.ndarray, np.ndarray, float]:
    """1-to-1 nearest-neighbour matching on the logit propensity score, with a caliper.

    Each joiner is paired with the non-joiner whose score is closest, if the two are within
    ``caliper_sd`` standard deviations of the logit score; a non-joiner may serve several joiners.
    This estimates the effect on the joiners (the ATT).

    Args:
        frame: Customers.
        ps: Propensity scores.
        caliper_sd: Largest allowed score gap, in standard deviations of the logit score.

    Returns:
        The row positions of the matched joiners, of their matched non-joiners, and the share of
        joiners that found a partner.
    """
    logit = np.log(ps / (1 - ps))
    is_t = frame["treated"].to_numpy() == 1
    t_idx, c_idx = np.flatnonzero(is_t), np.flatnonzero(~is_t)
    nn = NearestNeighbors(n_neighbors=1).fit(logit[c_idx].reshape(-1, 1))
    dist, pos = nn.kneighbors(logit[t_idx].reshape(-1, 1))
    ok = dist.ravel() <= caliper_sd * logit.std()
    return t_idx[ok], c_idx[pos.ravel()[ok]], float(ok.mean())


def smd(frame: pd.DataFrame, column: str, weights: np.ndarray | None = None) -> float:
    """Standardised mean difference between joiners and non-joiners (0 means balanced).

    Args:
        frame: Customers (``treated`` marks the joiners).
        column: The covariate.
        weights: Optional weights for the non-joiners.

    Returns:
        The difference in means divided by the pooled standard deviation.
    """
    t = frame.loc[frame["treated"] == 1, column]
    c = frame.loc[frame["treated"] == 0, column]
    c_mean = np.average(c, weights=weights) if weights is not None else c.mean()
    return float((t.mean() - c_mean) / np.sqrt((t.var() + c.var()) / 2))


def estimate_effects(frame: pd.DataFrame, columns: list[str]) -> dict[str, float]:
    """The effect of joining estimated four ways, each from the recorded covariates only.

    Args:
        frame: Customers.
        columns: The recorded covariates.

    Returns:
        Naive difference, regression adjustment, propensity-score matching (ATT) and
        inverse-probability weighting (ATE), in euros.
    """
    y, t = frame["spend"].to_numpy(), frame["treated"].to_numpy()
    out = {"naive difference": y[t == 1].mean() - y[t == 0].mean()}
    design = sm.add_constant(frame[["treated"] + columns].astype(float))
    out["regression adjustment"] = float(sm.OLS(y, design).fit().params["treated"])
    ps = propensity_scores(frame, columns)
    t_idx, c_idx, _ = match(frame, ps)
    out["propensity matching"] = float((y[t_idx] - y[c_idx]).mean())
    clipped = np.clip(ps, 0.02, 0.98)
    w = np.where(t == 1, 1 / clipped, 1 / (1 - clipped))
    out["inverse weighting"] = float(
        np.average(y[t == 1], weights=w[t == 1]) - np.average(y[t == 0], weights=w[t == 0])
    )
    return out

11. H4: do the adjusted estimates recover the truth?¶

What: estimate the programme's effect four ways: the naive difference; regression adjustment (compare spend while controlling for the covariates); propensity-score matching (the effect on the joiners) and inverse-probability weighting (each customer weighted by one over the chance of the treatment they got, which makes the groups alike). To measure the uncertainty we bootstrap the whole pipeline (resample the customers and repeat the propensity model and the matching 200 times). Why: the true answer is +5; a method that is wrong with a tight interval is worse than one with a wide interval that includes the truth.

12. H5: what if an important cause is not recorded?¶

What: add a hidden trait (think "loyalty affinity") that pushes both joining the programme and spending, and that is not in the data. We vary how strongly it drives joining (0 = not at all, up to 1.2), simulate 10 data sets at each strength, and compute every estimator from the recorded covariates only. We also show what happens if the hidden trait were recorded. Why: "we adjusted for everything we have" is only reassuring if what we have is everything that matters.

13. 🎯 Takeaways¶

  1. Plan the sample size first: detecting 10% → 11% needs 14,745 visitors per arm; halving the lift to detect quadruples it (57,756).
  2. A fixed-horizon test keeps its promise: 5.20% false positives (CI 4.62% to 5.85%) in 5,000 A/A tests; the power formula (80.0%) matches the simulation (80.1%).
  3. Small tests that win lie: at a quarter of the planned sample only 28.5% of experiments find the effect, and those that do report 1.8 times the true lift.
  4. Peeking breaks the error rate: checking 10 times raises false positives from 5% to 18.9%, and 50 times to 32.3%; Bonferroni fixes it at a price.
  5. Report an interval, not just a p-value: +1.00 points (CI +0.30 to +1.69) tells you how much to trust the result.
  6. Without randomisation the naive comparison can be nearly 4 times the truth (+19.7 against +5.0 euros) with a p-value below 1e-300.
  7. Adjustment works for what you recorded: regression, matching and weighting recover +5 (estimates +4.9, +4.7, +4.5), and matching reduced the age imbalance from 0.445 to 0.002.
  8. It cannot fix what you did not record: with a strong hidden cause all three adjusted methods are off by more than the effect itself.

14. ⚠️ Limitations¶

  • Everything is simulated. That is deliberate (we know the truth), but real data have messier confounding, missing values and effects that differ between customers.
  • A constant treatment effect: every customer gains +5 euros. With effects that vary, matching estimates the effect on the joiners and weighting the effect on everybody, and they would differ.
  • One metric, two arms, equal split: real tests have several metrics (which needs correcting for multiple looks at the data), unequal splits and novelty effects.
  • Peeking was handled by the simplest correction: Bonferroni is conservative; group-sequential and always-valid methods are more efficient.
  • The propensity model is a plain logistic regression, correct by construction here. In practice it can be misspecified (try more flexible models and check balance).
  • Matching with replacement and a caliper is one of many matching designs; the bootstrap interval for matching is itself approximate.
  • Overlap was good here. With poor overlap (some customers who almost never or almost always join) matching and weighting become unstable.
  • The hidden confounder sweep uses 10 data sets per setting, so the bias figures carry some noise.
